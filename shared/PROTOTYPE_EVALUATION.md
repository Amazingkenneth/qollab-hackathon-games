# Selecting a prototype from actual play

Use this as a team decision aid, not an official judging rubric. The purpose is to find the concept whose implemented interaction makes people infer something interesting and want to see the ending.

## Equal conditions

Give each prototype the same desktop viewport, headphones available but optional, a fresh start, and a six-to-eight-minute first-play limit. Do not explain the solution before the first attempt. Ask the player to say what they think a control changes. If possible, include one person who knows quantum mechanics and one who does not. Team members can perform the first pass when outside testers are unavailable; label that limitation.

Separate three kinds of evidence: observation (“the player switched basis without a hint”), interpretation (“the target icon may have made the clue clear”), and preference (“I liked the kitchen's tone”). Do not turn one enthusiastic reaction into a claim about all users. Do not fabricate playtest participants or scores.

## First-pass gates

Reject or repair a prototype before aesthetic ranking if its core model is wrong, the ending is unreachable, randomness can indefinitely block progression, controls misrepresent their effect, hidden information leaks, or the experience depends on unavailable accounts/assets. A correct greybox can be improved; a beautiful fake mechanism needs substantive repair.

## Score sheet

Use 1–5, where 1 means absent/confusing and 5 means consistently clear/compelling in the observed session. The suggested weights are original team-planning choices, not Qollab's weights.

| Dimension | Weight | Evidence to record |
|---|---:|---|
| Player inference | 25% | Can the player explain why the successful action works in their own words? |
| Memorable reveal | 20% | What exact moment would they describe to someone else? |
| Story/mechanic connection | 15% | Does the mechanism change the meaning of the story or only decorate it? |
| Scientific honesty | 15% | Are probabilities, uncertainty, records, and evidence represented accurately? |
| Visual/art potential | 10% | Would the team's original art materially improve the core experience? |
| Demonstration reliability | 10% | Can the ninety-second route work without rigging outcomes? |
| Remaining production effort | 5% | Are the remaining improvements feasible in the actual time left? |

Weighted total is sum(weight×score)/5 for a percentage-scale planning score. Record confidence and notes beside the number. Do not overinterpret one-point differences from a tiny playtest.

## Concept-specific observations

| Concept | Look for this evidence | Warning sign |
|---|---|---|
| Last Light | player distinguishes hiding a record from reversing it | they think looking at the UI itself destroys the beam |
| Palimpsest | player reads panel order and phase glyphs without opening a dense inspector | they solve by random card shuffling and cannot connect page to model |
| Ghostlight Museum | player calls Bright inconclusive and includes absorption in the cost | they believe the method is always risk-free |
| Memory Thief | player accepts ? as a justified successful action | they think 85.4% confidence means certainty or repeatedly retry until lucky |
| Coherence Kitchen | player changes taster basis to detect phase | they believe all matching Z histograms are equivalent dishes |
| Quantum Forgery | player distinguishes joint from individual-copy success | they think two physical outputs prove cloning |
| The Last Bridge | player checks the whole middle-population trace | they inspect only final population or treat probability as a literal half-person |

## Ask immediately after play

1. What did you change, and why did it work?
2. Which moment surprised you?
3. What did the game let you conclude, and what remained uncertain?
4. Where did you feel lost or start guessing?
5. Would you want another scene using this mechanic?

Record the answer before teaching the intended explanation. A polished debrief can hide a confusing game if you only ask after explaining it yourself.

## Choose and improve

Shortlist two concepts after the first pass. Give each one targeted repair addressing its largest observed problem, then repeat the same short test. Stop expanding the field once one prototype has a compelling reveal, a complete ending, and manageable remaining risk. If the top concept needs a structural rewrite while the runner-up needs only art and pacing, factor that into the decision.

For the chosen project, let the artists replace the most visible story assets first: central background, protagonist/reaction, signature device, and ending illustration. Preserve control readability and model-driven overlays. Finish by rehearsing the ninety-second route and checking the actual submission environment, credits, and evidence labels.
